  1. File 01 Primary

    Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution. The Triple Revolution. Santa Barbara, CA: Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution, 1964. https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/isr/vol25/no03/adhoc.html.

    — Annotation

    This 1964 memorandum was written by the Ad Hoc Committee on the Triple Revolution, a group of thirty-five scientists, intellectuals, and activists who sent the document to President Lyndon Johnson out of a “feeling of foreboding” about the nation’s economic and social trajectory. The committee argued that American society was undergoing three mutually reinforcing revolutions: the Cybernation Revolution (the combination of the computer and the automated, self-regulating machine), the Weaponry Revolution (nuclear arms), and the Human Rights Revolution. The memorandum’s central argument is that automation was creating a system of almost unlimited productive capacity that required increasingly less human labor, and that this transition was happening far faster than the agricultural and industrial revolutions. The authors warned that conventional economic analysis failed to address this rapid change because it incorrectly assumed that new jobs would naturally absorb displaced workers, and they proposed that the government legally guarantee an adequate income to every individual and family as a human right. For my research, this source was important as an alternative perspective to the optimism of AI researchers like Herbert Simon, who argued in The Shape of Automation (published one year later) that mass unemployment was unlikely and that automation would free humans for more creative work.

  2. File 02 Primary

    Dreyfus, Hubert. Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence. Santa Monica, CA: RAND Corporation, 1965. https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/papers/2006/P3244.pdf.

    — Annotation

    This is a paper from philosopher Hubert Dreyfus, and is an early critique of the symbolic, rule-based approach to AI prevalent after the Dartmouth Conference (often called GOFAI, or “Good Old-Fashioned AI”). Dreyfus argues that the early successes of AI led to the mistaken assumption that all intelligence activities can be “formulated in a program” and simulated on a digital computer. He examines the difficulties AI ran into with problems like pattern recognition and complex decision-making, concluding that the attempt to formalize intelligence “systematically excludes three fundamental human forms of information processing”: fringe consciousness, essence/accident discrimination, and ambiguity tolerance. His conclusion rejects the idea that digital, symbolic systems can achieve human intelligence, and argues that significant progress requires new types of hardware, the only existing prototype of which is the human brain. This article helped me understand another perspective on how philosophically, the approach of the researchers at the Dartmouth Conference and other early AI endeavors could have been overly optimistic.

  3. File 03 Secondary

    Edwards, Paul N. “Constructing Artificial Intelligence.” In The Closed World: Computers and the Politics of Discourse in Cold War America, 239–73. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1996.

    — Annotation

    In this book, science historian Paul Edwards challenges the standard narrative regarding the birth of AI (the history of the ideas that developed), arguing that AI was fundamentally shaped by the “closed world” political discourse of the Cold War and the practical needs of military command-and-control systems. Edwards details the shift from the hardware-focused models of cybernetics to the software-focused, symbolic processing of AI (embodied vs. disembodied minds). He argues that the development of high-level programming languages helped create the illusion of the computer as a logical “symbol system” independent of its machinery. Furthermore, Edwards describes how the field was sustained almost entirely by the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA), specifically the IPTO under J.C.R. Licklider. The military needed “decision support” for rapid battlefield management, which aligned with researchers’ desires for time-sharing and interactive computing. Edwards concludes that AI represents a “cyborg discourse,” where the integration of human logic and machine processing was intended to solve the chaotic problems of global political containment. This source helped me understand how the development of AI was fundamentally tied to the context of the Cold War (there was more to it than just researchers exploring the field), how military ideas and the AI researchers’ ideas were connected, and how this research was practically achieved (funding).

  5. File 05 Primary

    Lighthill, James. “Part I: Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey.” In Artificial Intelligence: A Paper Symposium, 1–22. London: Science Research Council, 1972.

    — Annotation

    This document, widely known as “The Lighthill Report,” was commissioned by the British Science Research Council to evaluate the state of AI research after its first two decades. Sir James Lighthill, a distinguished hydro-dynamicist and applied mathematician, offers a highly critical assessment of the field. He categorizes AI research into three areas: Advanced Automation (A), Computer-based CNS studies (C), and a “bridge” category of Building Robots (B). Lighthill argues that while categories A and C have valid, distinct scientific goals, category B – the pursuit of general-purpose robots and systems – has been a profound disappointment. His central argument rests on the “combinatorial explosion,” the phenomenon where the increase in complexity of a problem domain causes the computational power required to solve it to grow exponentially, making general intelligent behavior in real-world environments intractable for the computers of the time. He also discusses the psychological motivations of researchers, suggesting that the drive to create “human-like” robots is often rooted in emotion rather than scientific logic. This report is historically significant because it effectively dismantled the justification for funding “general” AI research in the UK, leading to a massive reduction in support for the field and ultimately causing the first AI Winter. For my research, this was useful as a significant historical document as well as describing the state of AI after two decades of its development. It also serves as a primary counter-argument to the enthusiasm of Newell and Simon, demonstrating the disconnect between theory and engineering reality.

    — Annotation

  8. File 08 Secondary

    Mindell, David A. Between Human and Machine: Feedback, Control, and Computing before Cybernetics. Baltimore: The Johns Hopkins University Press, 2002.

    — Annotation

    In this book, historian David Mindell explores the convergence of control engineering, communication theory, and computing during World War II, arguing that the field of cybernetics and the concept of digital computing emerged from specific military problems regarding anti-aircraft fire control. Mindell details the work of Norbert Wiener at MIT and George Stibitz at Bell Labs. He argues that Wiener’s formulation of cybernetics was in part a reaction to the failure of his “AA predictor” project. Alienated by the military’s secrecy, Wiener reframed his engineering work as a universal, civilian philosophy connecting biological and mechanical systems. Conversely, the book details Stibitz’s practical application of telephone relays to create digital computers, noting how Stibitz introduced the term “digital” to distinguish his discrete, pulse-based methods from continuous analog ones. Mindell concludes that the digital revolution was not just a shift in hardware, but a conceptual shift in how engineers represented the world: from physical analogies (modeling motion with motion) to mathematical abstraction (software). For my research, this source provided more historical context, going back in time to the 40s. Describing the technology from before AI was established as a field of research, this source gave me a better idea of how the theory and engineering prior to Turing’s paper and the Dartmouth conference led up to those events, and how it was influenced by the war.

  9. File 09 Primary

    Newell, Allen, and Herbert A. Simon. “The Logic Theory Machine—A Complex Information Processing System.” IRE Transactions on Information Theory 2, no. 3 (September 1956): 61–79. https://doi.org/10.1109/tit.1956.1056797. Accessed at https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/papers/2024/P868.pdf.

    — Annotation

    This is a 1956 paper by Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon (first presented at the Dartmouth Conference), which describes the Logic Theorist, the first computer program to simulate human-like problem-solving processes by proving theorems in symbolic logic (known as the first AI program). The Logic Theorist is presented as a model for “heuristic methods,” which are contrasted with systematic algorithms – heuristics sacrifice completeness (guaranteed solution) for efficiency and human-like problem-solving. LT’s success in proving theorems from Principia Mathematica established the Information Processing System paradigm as a viable approach to studying human cognition. This paper provided a different viewpoint from the other two – rather than contemplating the philosophical aspect of AI (and thus its claimed impossibility), it informed on a real feat of engineering that demonstrated the progress made at the Dartmouth Conference. It was very interesting for me to see how concrete concepts in symbolic logic were designed to represent human cognitive processes.

  11. File 11 Primary

    “Science: The Thinking Machine.” Time, January 23, 1950. https://time.com/archive/6796057/science-the-thinking-machine-2/.

    — Annotation

    This cover story from Time provides an overview of the state of computer science in 1950. It discusses the transition from mechanical calculators like the Harvard Mark I to electronic systems like the Mark III. The article explores the technical foundations of computing (binary arithmetic and vacuum tubes), and addresses the philosophical and societal implications of thinking machines. It shows the conflicting viewpoints of contemporary experts, contrasting the “conservative” views of Howard Aiken, who saw computers as just tools, with the more radical cybernetic theories of Norbert Wiener and Warren McCulloch, who drew parallels between electronic circuits and human brains. This source is historically significant, as it captures the early public anxiety and optimism regarding automation, predicting future applications in weather forecasting, economics, and automatic factories, while speculating on the potential for the human brain to become obsolete. This article was useful for me, as it was the source most accessible to the public I’ve read so far (it’s from Time), so I understood what the information the general public received on this issue was more.

  12. File 12 Primary

    Searle, John R. “Minds, Brains, and Programs.” Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3, no. 3 (September 1980): 417–57. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0140525x00005756.

    — Annotation

    In this article, philosopher John Searle introduces the famous Chinese Room thought experiment. This article is a direct challenge to the claims of “Strong AI,” which Searle defines as the view that an appropriately programmed computer doesn’t just simulate a mind but “really is a mind.” Searle uses his thought experiment, in which he follows a program to manipulate Chinese symbols and produce perfect answers without understanding them, to argue that a computer program is “purely formal” (syntactical) and can never be sufficient for “semantics” (genuine understanding). He concludes that intentionality (“aboutness”) is a biological product of the specific “causal powers” of the brain, not a feature of a formal program. This article is a widely cited critique in the philosophy of mind, directly addressing the claims of researchers like those at the Dartmouth conference, and was very useful for me in understanding the main arguments against the optimism demonstrated at Dartmouth in the early days of AI.

  13. File 13 Primary

    Simon, Herbert A. The Shape of Automation for Men and Management. New York: Harper & Row, 1965.

    — Annotation

    In this book, Nobel laureate Herbert Simon discusses the transition from operations research to what he calls “heuristic problem solving” (AI). Simon argues that early computing focused on numerical “programmed” decisions, while the “second revolution” in management involves automating “nonprogrammed” (based on judgement) decisions using computers as symbol manipulators. He describes the theory behind this with the “Information Processing System” hypothesis, claiming human thinking is a complex organization of simple symbolic processes. Simon provides concrete examples of this. He describes the “General Problem Solver” (GPS), which uses means-ends analysis to solve problems similarly to humans, in addition to programs for geometry, checkers, and chess. Simon also addresses the socioeconomic implications of automation. He argues that mass unemployment is unlikely due to capital constraints on the rate of adoption of new technologies, as well as the principle of comparative advantage. This source is important for my research, since it connects early AI (GPS) and economic predictions of the time, which counters some of what Dreyfus was saying about the limits of rule-based systems.

  15. File 15 Primary

    The Thinking Machine. Episode of Tomorrow. Produced by CBS News and Thomas Wolf, featuring David Wayne and Jerome B. Wiesner. 1961, United States broadcast documentary. 53:15. https://mitmuseum.mit.edu/collections/object/1990.058.003. (Link used: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cvOTKFXpvKA)

    — Annotation

    This television special, hosted by David Wayne, is a primary source documenting the state of AI research in the early 1960s. The episode features prominent figures including Jerome Wiesner, Claude Shannon, and Herbert Simon. It explores whether machines can truly think or only simulate thought. It provides visual demonstrations of early AI projects, including pattern recognition, logical problem solving, game playing, and creative writing. The program also explores cybernetics and neurophysiology, and it compares computer programming to biological instinct through experiments with frogs and child psychology. For my research, this source is useful in capturing the optimism of the era and giving a good idea of what a common American might think of the state of AI (this is a CBS episode, so relatively accessible) at the time. Someone watching this might come away believing that general-purpose intelligent robots were only 10 to 15 years away. This source contrasts with the skepticism in the Lighthill Report and Dreyfus’s critiques (prior reading logs).

  16. File 16 Primary

    Turing, Alan. “Computing Machinery and Intelligence.” Mind 59, no. 236 (October 1, 1950): 433–60. https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433.

    — Annotation

    This is the landmark essay written by Turing in 1950, introducing the question of whether machines can think and proposing the imitation game (now Turing Test) as a practical way to evaluate machine intelligence. Turing argued that if a machine could convincingly imitate human responses in conversation, it should be considered intelligent. He discussed possible objections to machine intelligence, and rebutted them systematically. The paper was foundational in artificial intelligence and cognitive science, reframing intelligence as something testable. This essay helped me understand what the academic understanding of machine intelligence was at the time, and how Turing contributed to it.

  17. File 17 Primary

    United Press International. “New Navy Device Learns By Doing.” The New York Times, July 7, 1958.

    — Annotation

    This 1958 newspaper article reports on the US Navy’s unveiling of the “Perceptron,” an electronic device designed by Dr. Frank Rosenblatt to simulate the learning processes of the human brain. The article demonstrates the extreme optimism of the time regarding AI – it reports claims that the machine would eventually be able to walk, talk, reproduce itself, and be conscious. It describes a demonstration where a simulation of the Perceptron (running on an IBM 704 computer) learned to distinguish between left and right after fifty trials, which was described as a “self-induced change in the wiring diagram.” This source was important for my research, as it was a newspaper article from the time that reflects the general optimism about AI. This article was also before the field shifted more toward symbolic AI. It illustrates the difference between a specific engineering achievement (recognizing left and right squares) and the ambitious theoretical promises (consciousness, self-replication) that would later lead to the first AI winter, as described in the Lighthill Report (Reading Log 1).
